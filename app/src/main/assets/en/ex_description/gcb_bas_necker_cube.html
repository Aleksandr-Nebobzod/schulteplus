Necker Cube

The exercise uses the space "Base". The main task here at the first stage is registering involuntary movements of the mind, and later reducing the influence of involuntariness on perception.

The Necker Cube is an optical illusion, first published in 1832 by Swiss crystallographer Louis Necker. It is an example of ambiguous drawings (Wikipedia). It is also described as an "unstable or non-equilibrium perceptual environment" -- a term used in psychonetics, including for ambiguous figures (see Bakhtiyarov Oleg Georgievich, "Active Consciousness", Chapter 4. Perceptual Environment Management).

So, in a flat set of lines, you managed to see a three-dimensional cube. And it will either appear as a top view or a bottom view. Keep observing this object, and typically after a few seconds, your brain will switch the perceived projection by itself. Without your will, without your effort...
What's going on?! Let's figure it out.

Instructions

This stimulus material offers rich opportunities for practical exercises. Below we provide some of them, and you can come up with others yourself. Please note that one session of training with the Necker Cube should not exceed 15 minutes[1].

  1. Projection Retention

    Observe the switching of Necker Cube projections, registering them by pressing the right button [Event] in the "Base" space. It's important to aim for strict adherence to the instruction, in this case, "the projection switched — press the button", any other mental content (complaints or joys, attempts to strain your vision, changing the pressing technique, commenting, etc.) are not part of the instruction. (Also try to avoid getting stuck on the "middle projection"—when the image is perceived not as a cube, but as a flat figure).

    The training log shows how many events occurred during the session, note this number day by day. Over time, the Necker Cube will behave more stably, and you will become aware of the subtle psy-organ used to switch it.[2].

  2. Projection Control

    When projection retention reaches an average of 10 seconds, you can start voluntary switching of the projections. Here are a couple of interesting techniques.

    • During prolonged retention of a projection, you can track a moment when it is about to switch on its own. This moment should be used to switch it independently, not allowing unclear conditioning to take control of our tasks. This is important.
    • Turn on a musical composition of moderate tempo, for example, "The Blue Danube" by J. Strauss. Choose a duration that suits you (for example, four bars) for holding one projection, switch to the other projection during the next four bars. Then gradually increase the interval duration to eight, sixteen bars, and so on.

  3. Simultaneous Projections

    After mastering sufficient retention and some control over the Necker Cube projections, we can attempt to "see the world as it is"[3]. To do this:

    • Achieve a stable upper projection
    • While maintaining the result in attention, detach part of the attention flow (you can test its functionality on other objects) and direct the second flow to the same stimulus material, but with the idea of the lower projection.
    The result, if successful, will be impossible to miss. It is the feeling of paradox, when two different objects exist in the same place (or, conversely, one observer sees from two points at the same time).

    There's also a more technological approach, using a virtual object (see the instructions on stereoscopic materials)

    • In normal perception mode, focus your gaze between the figures. Make the left cube the upper projection, and the right cube the lower projection.
    • Ensure the stability of the figures and gradually shift perception into stereoscopic mode.
    • Once the central cube stabilizes, determine its content. Both projections should be active (you can confirm this by checking that the left and right cubes continue to be interpreted differently).

  4. Projection Elimination

    This exercise can take between 15 to 30 minutes. Work is done using the apophatic technique, meaning, for each stable projection of the Necker Cube, we internally respond: "not that." You should work without effort, without emotions, without desires, methodically forming the intention to see another figure. At some point, the set of lines presented will no longer form any gestalt structure. This can be called a local cessation of consciousness.

    Another technique to achieve this result is morphing. By repeatedly observing transitions from one cube projection to another, you should slow this process down. At a certain moment, the speed of interpretation switching will decrease to such an extent that you can stop it exactly in the middle, when one projection is already deactivated, and the other is not yet activated.

At the end of the exercise, press the left button of the "Base" space and fill in the log entry.
Realize that despite the illusory nature of the objects in this world, we live in this world, interact with it, and can be productive and happy.

Summary

The first encounters with illusions allow us to perceive reality more flexibly, to notice subtle fluctuations of the mind more easily, and with a slight effort of will, direct our energy towards what we consider beneficial for ourselves and others.
Mastering the exercises with the Necker Cube provides the opportunity to switch perspectives in any world event and consciously choose an interpretation.
